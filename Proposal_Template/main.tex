\section{Abstract}
Maintaining consistent exercise habits over an extended period can be challenging,
particularly when fitness progress is primarily represented through numerical data.
MakeMe is a gamified fitness-tracking application designed to address this challenge by
integrating personalized avatar progression into the fitness-tracking experience.
The application translates users' real-world activities into visual changes in a personalized
virtual character, with its initial minimum viable product (MVP) incorporating four core
components: character customization, workout logging, muscle-specific progression,
and a 91-day game cycle. Character customization allows users to personalize their avatars,
establishing a stronger sense of connection with their virtual representations,
while the 91-day cycle introduces a structured progression period that provides a longer-term
objective. At the core of this system, workout logging records users' training activity,
which drives muscle-specific growth in their avatars. This creates a direct relationship
between real-world exercise and avatar development, allowing users to visualize
how their individual training activities contribute to their avatars progression.
Through these interconnected mechanics, MakeMe aims to provide users with a more engaging
representation of their fitness progress and encourage consistent exercise through
game-inspired progression mechanics.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Context}
Conventional fitness-tracking applications provide users with a range of tools to monitor
and manage their physical activities. Activities that were traditionally recorded through
handwritten logs and manual calculations can now be conveniently organized within digital
platforms, allowing users to track workout frequency, monitor performance, and review their 
progress over time. These features provide valuable feedback and help users establish a
structured approach to their fitness routines. Across many of these applications,
numerical statistics remain a central method of presenting users' progress. 
A review of 379 fitness applications identified metrics such as daily steps, exercise duration, 
and calorie expenditure among commonly used methods of measurement.\cite{knight2015public}
While these measurements provide useful information, they offer limited opportunities
for users to visually identify with their progress or develop an aspirational connection
with their fitness goals. As a result, the ability to monitor fitness activity does not
necessarily translate into sustained motivation or consistent exercise habits. 

One potential approach to making fitness progress more engaging is through the use of
personalized avatars. These avatars can serve as visual representations of users,
gradually developing alongside their real-world physical activities. By allowing users
to customize their avatars, applications can create opportunities for stronger
self-identification, making the avatar's development more personally meaningful.
Furthermore, allowing these characters to develop toward an idolized physique introduces
an aspirational element, giving users a visual representation of what they may hope to
achieve through their own fitness journey. Through this combination of self-representation
and aspiration, avatars can provide users with a more personal and engaging
connection to their fitness goals.

Building upon these concepts, MakeMe is a gamified fitness-tracking application designed
to connect users' real-world exercise activities with the development of a personalized
avatar. The initial minimum viable product (MVP) focuses on four core components:
character customization, workout logging, muscle-specific progression, and a 91-day game
cycle. Users can personalize their avatars and record their workout activities,
which contribute to the gradual development of specific muscle groups within the avatar.
Rather than representing physical development as a generalized progression, MakeMe aims
to reflect users' individual training habits through muscle-specific visual changes. This
progression is further structured around a 91-day cycle, providing users with a defined
period in which to develop their avatars and work toward their fitness goals. By combining
workout tracking, character customization, and visual progression, MakeMe seeks to create
a more personalized and game-inspired approach to monitoring fitness development.

\subsection{Motivation and Problem Statement}
Despite the availability of fitness-tracking applications, maintaining consistent
engagement with exercise routines remains a challenge for many individuals. Recording
workouts and monitoring physical activity can help users organize their fitness routines,
but these activities alone do not necessarily provide a compelling reason to remain engaged
over an extended period. There is an opportunity to explore how game-inspired progression
systems can make continued participation more meaningful by connecting users' exercise
efforts with a sense of personal development. The challenge addressed by MakeMe is to design
an application that translates individual workout activities into personalized,
muscle-specific avatar progression while providing a structured experience through its
91-day game cycle.

The motivation behind developing MakeMe stems from the opportunity to make fitness
tracking more engaging through concepts commonly found in video games.
By incorporating personalized character development into the fitness-tracking
experience, the project explores how users can establish a stronger connection
with their fitness goals through virtual self-representation and aspirational
progression. Rather than focusing exclusively on recording physical activities,
MakeMe aims to provide users with a visual representation of their training efforts,
where individual workouts contribute to the gradual development of a character
they have personalized. This approach seeks to make fitness progression more
personally meaningful while introducing game-inspired mechanics that may
encourage users to remain engaged with their exercise routines. Ultimately,
the project aims to explore how principles of character customization and progression
can be applied to fitness applications to support a more engaging approach to exercise tracking.
\subsection{Project Goals and Objectives}
MakeMe's primary goal is to explore how conventional fitness-tracking functionality
can be extended through game-inspired character progression. By incorporating personalized
virtual representations into the fitness-tracking experience, the project aims to establish
a more engaging approach to visualizing users' fitness development. The initial MVP serves
as the foundation for this broader vision, establishing the essential functionality and
progression systems upon which additional features can be developed and integrated in the future.

To establish this foundation, the project pursues the following objectives.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop a Personalized Avatar System: Implement a customizable virtual
    character whose appearance can be personalized by users with individual muscle groups
    that develops independently through recorded workout activities.
    
    \item Integrate Core Fitness-Tracking Functionality: Implement essential
    workout-logging functionality that allows users to record their exercise
    activities serving as the foundation for the application's avatar progression system.

    \item Establish a Structured Progression System: Develop a 91-day progression
    cycle that provides users with a defined period to track their activities, observe
    avatar development, and work toward longer-term fitness goals. User's can ascend into a new cycle
    through either conditions: completing the full 91-day cycle or reaching maximum growth across all
    muscles in their avatar. This design rewards users who achieve maximum avatar progression a shorter cycle.
    Upon ascension, a snapshot of the avatar will be recorded following with a badge as reward.

    \item Implement Persistent Data Storage: Develop a data storage system that preserves avatar configurations, 
    muscle-growth values, recorded workouts, progression cycle information, earned badges, and historical
    records of avatars. This will allow users to retain their progress across application sessions and review their
    development over multiple cycles.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review and Related Work}

\subsection{Relevant Research and Existing Products}
MakeMe’s avatar system draws on virtual self-modeling through vicarious reinforcement by Fox
and Bailenson, which explored how observing virtual representation of oneself receiving reinforcement
would influence exercise behavior \cite{fox2009VRS}. However, their research used virtual representation that
resembled the participants, whereas MakeMe features fantasy-based character development that would
later skew away from the users physical appearance. This distinction is intentional, as MakeMe allows
users to watch their avatars develop into increasingly muscular and powerful characters, potentially
inspiring them to aspire to the strength and appearance of their virtual counterparts, motivating them
to maintain healthy habits. This design aligns the concept of wishful identification mentioned in the
review research by Praetorius and Gorlich on the Proteus Effect, which describes how an avatar's characteristics
may influence a users behavior and self-perception \cite{praetorius2020avatars}.Drawing on these concepts, MakeMe’s
avatar system is designed around two complementary forms of progression: reflective and aspirational. Where the development
of the avatar is a direct reflection of users actions, while aspirational progression allows the avatar to evolve towards
an idolized physique that users may wish to achieve themselves. While existing research does not directly establish
a link between avatar progression and the development of healthy habits, incorporating them may still offer
motivational benefits compared with health tracking applications without an avatar.

Although ranking of the top 10 fitness trackers may vary due to subjective
reviews and potential biases, many of them can be broadly classified into three categories based on their approaches
to fitness tracking and gamification.

Category 1 consists of conventional fitness trackers that incorporate little to no gamification,
focusing primarily on workouts, activity logging, and social features \cite{hall2026bestfitnessapps, herman2026bestfitnessapps}. Their
popularity suggests that stand-alone fitness monitoring remains an established approach to helping users
track their progress and maintain their fitness routine. However, this category represents only one approach
to fitness tracking and does not establish whether users would benefit from engaging or game-oriented alternatives.

Category 2 consists of challenge-based gamified fitness trackers that are oriented to encourage physical
activity through step-count goals, competitions, social challenges, and real-world incentives. In 2020, (Neupane et al.)
reviewed 103 gamified fitness tracker apps and found that challenges, competition and social influence frequently
appeared together, while social features played a central role across nearly every identified game genre \cite{neupane2020review}.
These findings demonstrate how conventional activity tracking can be enhanced with game mechanics that encourage
users to remain active through external rewards, social interaction, and competition. However, this approach primarily
builds on existing fitness tracking activities rather than making exercise part of a broader, evolving game experience.

Category 3 consists of stand-alone games that integrate physical activity directly into gameplay, encouraging
users to exercise as they pursue in-game objectives. Games such as PokemonGO and Wii Sports demonstrate the popularity
of this approach making physical activity more engaging through exploration and interactive gameplay \cite{koh2026motivational, yim2007using}.
In these games, physical activity is not simply a means of tracking progress but an integral part of the game itself. Players are motivated to move, explore, and engage with their environment to achieve in-game goals.
However, research on PokemonGO suggests that increases in physical activity may be short-lived, highlighting the challenge
of sustaining user engagement and long-term exercise habits.

\subsubsection{91-day Progression Cycle Rationale}
Physical development through resistance training is a gradual process influenced by several factors, including workout
consistency, training intensity, nutritional intake, and recovery. Research by Currier et al.(2023) provides a scientific
foundation for this relationship through a systematic review and Bayesion network meta-analysis examining the effects
of different resistance training prescriptions on muscle strength and hypertrophy.\cite{currier2023resistance} Their results suggest that a range
of training apporaches can improve both outcomes. This supports the idea that physical development comes from the accumulation
of many sessions over an extended period rather than from a single workout. Furthermore, these adaptations can be
supported in Ahtiainen et al. (2003), which examined the effects of a 21-week strength-training intervention on previously
untrained individuals and experienced stregth athletes.\cite{ahtiainen2003muscle} The result showed both groups experienced improvements in muscular
strength, although the untrained participants demonstrated greater improvements alongside significant increase in muscle
cross-sectional area. These findings illustrate how an individual's training background can influence how their body
responds to resistance training. Together, these studies demonstrate that resistance training can improve muscular
strength and hypertophy over time, while acknowledging that results may vary depending on an individual's training
background.

In addition, physical development is not determined by exercise alone. Among many factors, nutriional practices, particularly the management of
caloric intake, also play a role in how individuals approach muscle development and cahnges in body composition. Giannopoulos et al.
examined nutrition in a 24-week pilot intervention inspired by bodybuilding practices, in which a 12-week caloric-surplus
phase was followed by a 12-week caloric-restriction phase alongside resistance training.\cite{giannopoulos2025bulkcut} The study was limited in
scale, therefore it is used as an example of how training and nutrition can be organized into structured periods when
pursuing changes in physical development. Although the MVP of MakeMe does not track nutrition, the example shows that 
structured periods of several months are a familiar way to organize physical development.

Beyond the scientific considerations, similar periods are also reflected in the broader fitness
and bodybuilding community. 12-week programs and 3 month transformation challenges are commonly
presented as a way to set a goal and work toward visible changes. This can be observed
in many fitness-related publications and promotional materials, including a 90-day Transformation Challenge
featured by Men's Health\cite{MH2022transformation}, 12-week Fitness Transformation Program from Colfax Strong\cite{pinther2025fitness},
and a 3-month trial promotion in Gold's Gym Harajuku.\cite{goldsgym2026trial}Although it is short, these transformations
often emphasize intense routines, consistency in exercise, strict nutrional management,
and recovery throughout the designated period. While such programs do not guarantee a particular
result, their popularity suggests that a period of about 3 months is a familiar way of approaching fitness
goals for MakeMe's intended users.

The length of the cycle must also be considered from a game-design perspective. A long cycle gives more
time for physical development, but it also lengthens the time before a user reaches a major milestone.
For an application with a small number of gameplay mechanics, a very long cycle could make progression
feel repitive and weaken the sense of achievement associated with reaching new milestones. Conversely,
a cycle that is too short may not give users enough time to log workouts and observe meaningful changes
in their avatars. Therefore, MakeMe adopts a 91-day progression cycle as a design compromise, providing
a defined period for gradual avatar development while keeping progression milestones within a more
manageable timeframe.

Ascension is the final consideration in the progression system. Upon completing a cycle, users are
intended to begin a new progression cycle by resetting their active avatar and completing the initial survey
again. This allows the avatar's starting configuration to reflect potential changes in the user's
physical development since their previous cycle. For example, a user who selected a skinny build in the
first cycle may select an average build in the next cycle. For users who begins with more developed
physiques, such as an athletic build, future expansion of fantasy-based progression would 
introduce additional build categories that allow their avatars to continue developing beyond realistic
physical representations. The previous avatar is intended to be preserved as records of avatar so that users can review their development across completed cycles. 

\subsection{Comparison With the Proposed Project}
Initially, MakeMe was envisioned as an application that would combine elements from the three
approaches discussed above, bringing together conventional fitness tracking, gamification,
and interactive gameplay. However, as the project developed, its scope was refined to focus
on a more specific aspect of the fitness-tracking experience: connecting users' workout
activities with the development of their avatars. Rather than attempting to incorporate
the various challenges, social features, and gameplay mechanics found in existing solutions,
the current MVP places its emphasis on workout logging and muscle-specific avatar progression.
Through this approach, recorded exercise activities directly contribute to the development of
corresponding muscle groups within a personalized character. This shift allows MakeMe to
concentrate on its central design concept providing users with a visual representation of
their training efforts through an avatar that develops alongside their fitness journey.

\section{Project Requirements}
\subsection{Product Vision and Scope}
MakeMe is a gamified fitness-tracking application that aims to connect users’ real-world fitness
activities to a developing personalized avatar. The application is designed around the idea that
progress from workouts can be represented through a character that gradually develops alongside
the user’s training habits. Rather than relying exclusively on numerical workout records, MakeMe
complements them with visual character development to provide users with another way to observe
their fitness progress.

The MVP focuses on four main components: character customization, workout logging, muscle-specific
avatar progression, and a 91-day progression cycle. Users can personalize their avatar upon
creation before recording their workouts. Based on the recorded workouts, the avatars
corresponding muscle groups gradually develops, allowing different areas of the character
to progress according to the user’s training habits. The 91-day cycle provides a structured
period for users to work toward their fitness goals, with the opportunity to ascend into a
new cycle, receiving badges to recognize their accomplishments.

The original conceived idea was a broader system that considered nutrition, hydration, and sleep
tracking, alongside additional mechanics such as avatar decay, recovery, and bulk progression.
However, these features have been excluded from the initial MVP due to the interest of time and
the amount of resources they required. Several of the proposed systems also depended on extensive
visual assets and additional implementation, which would significantly increase the project’s
development complexity. Ultimately, MakeMe concluded to prioritize the core workout and avatar
progression mechanics before considering integrating additional features. This narrower scope
allowed the project to concentrate on its primary mechanic while establishing a foundation for
future expansion.

\section{System Design}
\subsection{System Architecture}
The application is developed using Flutter and Dart, with Provider for state management
and coordinating UI updates. For development and testing, Android Studio provides an integrated
environment in which the application can be deployed and run through an Android emulator,
allowing the team to evaluate its functionality and visual presentation. Its architecture
emphasizes modularity and separation of responsibilities, utilizing immutable data models
and a layered sprite rendering system to support customizable avatars and muscle-specific
visual progression.

The UserProfile class is at the center of this design. It owns the user's avatar and is related to one or
more avatars, so earlier avatars can be kept as a history once a cycle ends. The avatar class is immutable
, so methods such as copyWith() and addGrowth() return a new Avatar instead of changing the existing
one, which allows the interface to detect a change and redraw the avatar. Each avatar has many
WorkoutLog entries, and each log determines how much growth a session contributes to the muscle groups
trained. The CycleManager class tracks the 91-day cycle, and the ScreenManager class handles navigation between
screens. The AvatarDisplay widget draws the current Avatar by stacking sprite layers whose file paths are built by
AssetPaths. Workout logging records each muscle separately through BodyPartName, including the biceps and
triceps, while each body part maps to one of the ten visual regions in AvatarRegion. As a result, the biceps
and triceps both contribute to the single upper-arm layer. The Avatar also stores a buildProgress value for
the skinny and heavy starting builds, and its sharedLevel() method calculates the shared muscle level used
to select each region's sprite, as described in the Avatar System section.

The sequence begins when the user logs a workout on the workout logger screen. After confirming the workout,
WorkoutLog calculates the growth amount from the duration and intensity of the session. For each body part,
this amount is passed to the UserProfile, which then calls Avatar to apply the growth. Because Avatar is
immutable, it returns a new Avatar with updated values. The UserProfile then completes two actions,
pass the updated Avatar to the CycleManager to check if the cycle is complete and notify the listeners
to update the widget AvatarDisplay to redraw the avatar. If the cycle completes the user is taken to the ascension screen
and is offered to start a new cycle.

\subsection{Design Patterns and Justification for Their Use}
MakeMe uses three design patterns, one of them, Observer, is part of the classical list of patterns
from Gang of Four book, while the other two, Immutable Object and Dependency Injection, are common practices
in object-oriented programming.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Observer: The UserProfile and ScreenManager classes extend ChangeNotifier, and the widgets
        that depend on them subscribe through Provider. When the data changes, the class calls notifyListeners(),
        and only the widgets watching it are rebuilt. MakeMe chose this pattern because the avatar needs to update
        on screen every time a workout is logged, and having each screen check for changes would be repetitive.
        With the Observer pattern, the model does not need to know which screens are displaying it.
    \item Immutable Object: The Avatar and HeadConfig classes are never modified in place. Methods such as
        copyWith(), addGrowth(), withOption() return a new object with the changed values while retaining the
        unchanged values. This works well alongside the Observer pattern, since a change to the avatar produces
        a new object, which makes it clear when the display needs to redraw. It also keeps the avatar's data
        in one place and reduces the chance of a program unexpectedly changing it. The trade-off is that a
        new object is created on every change, but once replaced Dart's garbage collector can reclaim it.
        Therefore, the old copies never accumulate in memory.
    \item Dependency Injection: The managers are created once in app.dart via MultiProvider, and screens
        retrieve them with context.read instead of creating their own. This means every screen works with the same UserProfile
        and the same ScreenManager, so the avatar a user creates is the same one that appears on the home screen.
        It also keeps the screens simpler, since they do not need to know how the managers are built.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation and Proof of Concept}

\subsection{Tools and Technologies}

The front-end interface of MakeMe was developed using Flutter and the Dart programming language. Flutter provides a widget-based framework for building mobile application interfaces.

Android Studio and its Android emulator were used to display and inspect the application during development. Git and GitHub were used for version control and collaboration.
The application also uses the Provider package for state management. A ScreenManager class, which extends ChangeNotifier, maintains the currently selected screen.
When a user presses a navigation button, the setScreen() method updates the current screen and notifies the application to display the corresponding interface.

The avatar's visual assets were created in Blender \cite{blender} with MPFB2 \cite{mpfb2}, a plugin that generates human models, and Python scripts were used to automate the rendering of the avatar's sprite layers.

\subsection{Implementation of the Main Features}

\subsubsection{Front-End Screens and Navigation}

\textbf{Navigation Implementation}

Screen navigation is managed through the ScreenManager class and the Screens enumeration. Each screen accesses the ScreenManager through Provider and calls setScreen() when a navigation button is pressed.
This approach separates navigation state from individual screen layouts and allows the application to switch between screens without duplicating navigation logic.

\subsubsection*{Workout Log}
The workout log screen implements requirement R2. It lets the user enter the three inputs the avatar growth calculation depends on:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Duration:} the length of the workout.
  \item \textbf{Muscle group:} the muscle group trained, selected from a fixed list.
  \item \textbf{Intensity level:} how demanding the workout was.
\end{itemize}
Using selection controls for the muscle group and intensity keeps the input consistent and avoids invalid values.
When the user confirms an entry, the screen passes the logged data to the growth logic (R3), 
which applies growth to the corresponding muscles, and the user returns to the avatar screen, where the updated appearance is displayed (R4).

\subsubsection*{Workout Log Template}
Entering the same workout repeatedly is tedious, which works against the goal of building a consistent habit, a need shared by all three personas. 
To reduce this effort, I implemented workout log templates. A template stores a combination of muscle group, duration, and intensity that the user performs regularly. 
Selecting a template fills in the workout log form, so a routine workout can be logged in a few taps. 
The user can still adjust any value before confirming, so a template acts as a starting point rather than a fixed entry.

\section{Required Resources and Feasibility}

\subsection{Hardware, Software, and Other Resources}
The front-end development requires a computer capable of running Flutter development tools and an Android emulator. The main software resources include Flutter, Dart, Android Studio, Git, and GitHub.
Flutter and Dart are used to develop the application interface, while Android Studio provides an environment for running and inspecting the application. Git and GitHub support collaborative development by allowing team members to maintain separate branches, track code changes, and integrate their contributions. The Provider package is used to manage application navigation state.

The avatar system requires additional resources to create its visual assets. Blender \cite{blender} and the MPFB2 plugin \cite{mpfb2} are used to model the avatar and render its sprites. Both are free and open source: MPFB2 is distributed under the GPL, and MakeHuman's core assets are released under CC0 \cite{makehumanlicense}, so they can be used in MakeMe without licensing costs. The asset pipeline's scripts are written in Python using Blender's built-in Python API and NumPy, so no additional software is required. Rendering the sprites requires a computer capable of running Blender, as each gender needs approximately 100 muscle sprites in addition to the build and face layers, each exported at three pixel densities. Within the application, the avatar's data and progression logic are implemented in Dart in the Avatar and UserProfile classes, and the shared\_preferences and sqflite packages are included in the project for storing user and avatar data locally on the device.

\subsection{Feasibility Within the Available
Time and Resources}

\paragraph{Workout Logging and Growth}
A WorkoutLogEntry requires the fields: duration, intensity and a list of BodyPartName provided by user input in the Workout
Log screen. Once the workout is saved, the screen calls UserProfile to execute a series of operations. First, it constructs
a WorkoutLogEntry using the provided fields and adds it into the workout history. Second, the entry calculates itself using 
Growth Calculator’s formula and converts the result into a map with their corresponding growth values. UserProfile reads this map, 
applies the additional growth and replaces the avatar’s map of BodyParts. The avatar is then passed to CycleManager and checks whether it has reached 
its maximum growth. Finally, changed() is called to save the updated data to disk asynchronously and triggers a UI redraw.

The growth formula is duration / 30 * intensity multiplier (light 0.5, moderate 1.0, and heavy 1.5). The resulting growth value
is then distributed evenly across the selected muscle groups by dividing the total growth by the number of selected muscles. 
The user’s selected weekly goal determines the target growth required for every muscle to reach its maximum. Consequently, 
users who train harder, for longer durations, or more frequently will progress faster and ascend earlier.


\paragraph{Progression Cycle and Ascension}
Throughout the cycle, whenever the avatar changes, UserProfile passes the updated avatar to CycleManager to determine whether
the avatar is eligible to ascend. Ascension becomes available when all muscles have reached their maximum growth, or the user
has completed the full 91-day cycle. After meeting one of these conditions, the application prompts the user to select
“Ascend” to proceed. Upon ascension, the avatar is converted into an AvatarRecord that contains a week met / total week
summary highlighting the number of weeks they were consistent with their weekly goal compared to the total cycle. Each
AvatarRecord is displayed as a card in avatar selection screen, using the saved avatar configuration to display as preview
of the previous avatars. Ascension also awards a 16-tiered badge that evolves with each ascension and is carried over to
the new avatar. The badge is displayed in avatar selection screen, where it serves as an entry point to the home screen and
alongside the avatar on the home screen.

\paragraph{Persistence}
All user data such as avatar, head configurations, avatar records, workout history, templates, ascension level,
and cycle start date is consolidated into a UserData snapshot, with each component supporting toJson and fromJson
conversion to represent the UserProfile’s current state. StorageService saves and loads this snapshot into one JSON
string using SharedPreferencesAsync, which stores data using key-value storage locally on disk. On web-builds,
browser storage is kept separately for each origin (address and port). Data is saved asynchronously after every change
and loaded once at startup. If the saved avatar cannot be read or the JSON data is corrupted, the application resets
to an empty profile and the damaged save will be overwritten at the next save.

\subsubsection{Avatar System}

The avatar system turns a user's muscle-specific progress into a visible character. The avatar is drawn as a stack of 2D sprite layers, with one layer for each body region, so that each region can change independently based on the workouts the user logs.

\paragraph{Asset Creation.}
The avatar's body was modeled in Blender using MPFB2 \cite{blender, mpfb2}, starting from MakeHuman body variants for each gender: skinny, average, heavy, and three muscle levels. These variants were combined into a single adjustable model, and its surface was divided into ten muscle regions (abs, calves, forearms, glutes, lats, pectorals, quadriceps, shoulders, trapezius, and upper arm), along with the head, hands, and feet. The nose and mouth presets used for avatar customization were created from MPFB2's built-in face shapes. Python scripts in Blender automate the process, so the full set of sprites can be regenerated whenever the model changes.

\paragraph{Builds and Muscle Levels.}
Following R1, the initial survey determines the avatar's starting build: skinny, average, athletic, or heavy. A skinny or heavy avatar transitions gradually to the average build before any muscle growth becomes visible, while an athletic avatar begins at the first muscle level in every region. Each region then grows through four levels, from 0 (average) to 3 (large). Workouts are logged by individual muscle, so the biceps and triceps are tracked separately but combined into the single upper-arm region for display.

\paragraph{Seamless Region Borders.}
Because each region is drawn as a separate layer, neighboring layers must line up exactly where they meet. Early tests that held every border at the average body shape produced visible pinching between regions as the muscles grew. To avoid this, the application tracks a shared level $g$, defined as the lowest level among all regions. Every border is rendered at this shared level, while each region's interior grows to its own level $s$. As a result, neighboring layers always align, and an avatar that grows evenly matches the original model exactly. This approach requires one sprite for each combination of shared level and region level, which is ten sprites per region and 100 muscle sprites per gender.

\paragraph{Rendering and Application Integration.}
The sprites are rendered by a Python script in Blender from a fixed orthographic camera, with the avatar in an A-pose so that the arms do not overlap the torso in the front view. Each layer is rendered with every other region masked out, in grayscale with a transparent background, at a resolution of $1200 \times 2400$ pixels. Each layer is exported at three pixel densities ($600 \times 1200$, $800 \times 1600$, and $1200 \times 2400$) so that Flutter can load the version that best matches the device's screen \cite{flutterassets}. Files follow the naming scheme \texttt{\{gender\}\_\{region\}\_g\{g\}\_s\{s\}.png}, which allows the asset path for any combination of levels to be built in code.

In the application, the AvatarDisplay widget stacks the layers from back to front, and the AssetPaths helper builds each file path. Because the sprites are rendered in grayscale, the user's skin tone is applied at runtime by tinting each layer with Flutter's \texttt{BlendMode.modulate}, so a single set of sprites supports any skin tone. Growth values between two levels are displayed by fading between neighboring sprites, which AvatarDisplay already supports. To complete the integration, the Avatar model must also calculate the shared level $g$ and select each region's sprite by its pair of levels.

\subsubsection{Proof of Concept}
The team has added debugging tools in the profile menu intended for verification and demonstration, and not
as user features. The growth simulator helps visualize the avatar progression throughout the 91-day cycle for
each build. Its drop-down selection and goal slider also visualizes the growth calculation that determines
the three cases for users who are: underperforming, over performing, or hitting their goals for ascension.
“Force End Cycle” and “Force Maxed Avatar” verify that a completed full 91-day cycle and reaching the maximum
growth for all muscles triggers an ascension and ends the cycle.

\subsubsection*{Growth Calibration Tests}

The most important tests check that the pace of growth works as designed (Section~9.5). They run the real application code through \texttt{GrowthSimulator}, replaying 91 days for simulated users. They check the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A user who follows the weekly goal maxes every build inside the cycle.
  \item A user who trains more than the goal maxes earlier, but not immediately.
  \item A user who trains less than the goal never maxes, and ascends on day 91.
  \item Nobody can ascend on day 0.
  \item For every combination of the four builds, three simulated users and three weekly goals (1, 3 and 7), the avatar stays valid on every day of the cycle.
  \item Skinny and heavy avatars pass through both build stages before any muscle grows, and average and athletic avatars never show them.
  \item Thirteen weeks of three sessions make 39 workouts, with none on day 91.
\end{itemize}

These tests turned the design goal of the growth model into something that can be checked after each change. When we changed a constant of the growth model, the calibration tests showed immediately whether one of the three cases had been broken.

\subsubsection*{Manual Testing}

The screens and the navigation have no automated tests. We checked them manually on the Android emulator, going through the main flow: creating an avatar, logging a workout (with and without a template), watching the avatar change, and opening the status and avatar selection screens. To reach the ascension without waiting 91 days, we used ``Force Maxed Avatar'' and ``Force End Cycle'' from the profile menu, which show that both conditions end the cycle and open the ascension. We also restarted the application after each of these steps to check that the data was kept.

\subsubsection*{Discussion of Results}

The automated tests cover the parts of the application where an error would be hardest to notice: the growth, the cycle, the ascension and the storage. They are also the parts where the application would lose the user's data if they were wrong. The tests have the following limits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item They check the rules, not the layout, so a wrong position of a sprite layer or a broken button would not be found.
  \item The calibration tests use simulated users, so they show that the model behaves as designed, not that real users find the pace motivating.
  \item The application has not yet been tested with real users over a full 91-day cycle.
\end{itemize}
Testing with real users, and tests for the screens, are listed as future work in Section~10.2.
\subsection{Challenges, Limitations, and Reasons for Incomplete Features}

\subsubsection{Challenges}

\paragraph{Separating the workout log from the avatar's appearance (R2, R3, R4).}
A workout log has to be saved exactly as the user entered it, but it should not be mixed with the logic that draws the avatar. The same applied to templates. \texttt{WorkoutLogEntry} therefore stores only the user's inputs, \texttt{GrowthCalculator} calculates the growth from them, \texttt{UserProfile} applies it to the avatar, and the display code never reads the workouts. The growth rules can then be changed without rewriting saved workouts.

\paragraph{Making growth fair for every build and goal (R3).}
Requirement R3 says that the system must calculate growth, but it does not say how fast an avatar should grow. If growth is too fast, users max out their avatar in a few weeks. If it is too slow, a user who follows their own goal never sees the avatar complete. We therefore tied the growth needed to reach the highest level to the user's weekly goal, and checked the result by replaying the whole 91-day cycle with simulated users (\texttt{GrowthSimulator}) for every build and goal. This showed that users who meet their goal max their avatar inside the cycle, users who train more max earlier, and users who train less never max and ascend at day 91.

\paragraph{Keeping the stored data consistent (R3, R4).}
We store the raw growth of each muscle, which only ever increases, and translate it into a displayed level when the avatar is drawn (\texttt{AvatarSprites}). The appearance can therefore be retuned without rewriting the user's saved data.

\paragraph{Layered avatar rendering (R1, R4).}
Because each body region is a separate sprite layer, neighbouring layers must line up exactly, and early tests produced visible pinching between regions. The shared level $g$ solved this (Section~7.2.1), but it means that every pair of shared level and region level needs its own sprite. The number of sprites made the rendering time and the size of the application the main practical concern of the asset pipeline.

\paragraph{When a cycle ends (R5, R6).}
An ascension must become available on exactly day 91, not on day 90, and also when the user does not open a workout for a long time. We therefore check the length of the cycle against the real clock, not only when the avatar changes, and we wrote tests for the days around the end of the cycle.

\paragraph{Screen navigation and shared state.}
With ten screens, we needed navigation that does not duplicate logic in each screen. \texttt{ScreenManager} handles this, and together with the back-gesture handler of the application it keeps a stray swipe on the home or login screen from closing the application. State shared between screens, such as a template chosen for the next workout, raised its own problem. The chosen template is kept in memory in \texttt{UserProfile} and handed over once by \texttt{takePendingTemplate()}. This method deliberately does not notify listeners, because it is called while the workout log screen is being built, and notifying at that point causes a Flutter error.

\paragraph{Reliable saving and loading (R8).}
All user data is saved as one JSON string, so damaged data must not prevent the application from starting. Loading is therefore defensive: unreadable data gives an empty profile, a single unreadable history record or workout is skipped while the others are kept, and out-of-range values are clamped. Saving runs in the background after every change, using a snapshot that is built immediately, so that a later change cannot affect a save in progress.

\paragraph{Collaboration and version control.}
Working with branches and pull requests on GitHub was new to some team members. For example, we learned that files can be added to or removed from a commit while the pull request is still open. Keeping the models, services, screens and widgets in separate files let team members work on different parts with fewer conflicts.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Simplified workout data (R2, R3).}
A workout records only the duration, the muscle groups and the intensity. Exercises, sets, repetitions and weights are not recorded, and the total growth is divided equally between the selected muscles. Avatar growth is therefore an estimate of training time and effort, not a measure of the real training load.

\paragraph{Fixed growth model (R3).}
The growth formula and the target growth per weekly goal are fixed in the code. They were calibrated with simulated users, not with data from real users over several months, so the pace of progression has not yet been validated with real users.

\paragraph{Limited customisation and rewards (R1, R7).}
Customisation is limited to a predefined set of head options, colours and four starting builds, because every combination needs its own sprites. There are 16 badges in total, which is also the highest ascension level. At the last level, the avatar can still ascend, but the level does not increase.

\paragraph{Local storage only (R8).}
All data is stored on the device with \texttt{SharedPreferencesAsync}. It is not synchronised between devices, and it is lost if the application data is cleared. On web builds, the data is also stored separately for each address and port. The login screen has no authentication: ``Start'' leads directly to the avatar selection screen, and ``Sign up'' leads to a placeholder screen.

\subsubsection{Incomplete Features}

The features below were part of the original proposal but are not among the high-level requirements R1--R8 and are not in the MVP (Table~1). Most were dropped because of the limited time and because they needed large amounts of additional visual assets, as explained in Section~4.1.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Avatar decay, avatar state system, bulk progression, and recovery and revival.} Each of these would need many extra sprites for the new appearances (for example a decayed, recovering or bulked avatar). Making these assets was not possible within the project's seven-week timeline. A 3D model would make them easier to add (Section~9.2).
  \item \textbf{Habit logging (meals, hydration, sleep).} The habit logger screen still shows buttons for these logs, but they lead to a placeholder screen. They were left out because there was no clear design for how nutrition, hydration and sleep should change the avatar, and the MVP focuses on workouts. This matters especially for users like Persona~2, who would like to track more habits in one application.
  \item \textbf{Workout streak tracking.} It does not affect muscle growth, so it was not needed for the core cycle of logging workouts, growth and ascension, and it is planned as future work.
  \item \textbf{Cycle completion summary and other rewards.} The detailed summary was reduced to the number of weeks in which the weekly goal was met, and only badges were implemented as rewards.
  \item \textbf{User accounts and synchronisation.} These are not part of R1--R8, so the application works for one user on one device.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transition to a 3D Model:} Replacing the current 2D sprite-based avatar system with a 3D character

    \item \textbf{Expand Progression:} Introduce additional progression paths, avatar builds and fantasy-based
        development stages. This would allow users to continue developing their characters beyond realistic
        physical representations, providing additional long-term progression.
    \item \textbf{Habit Loggers:} Implement habit-tracking features, such as nutrition, hydration, and sleep
        tracking, to restore the broader vision of MakeMe. The application’s primary goal is to extend
        conventional fitness application functionality through a visual layer of personalized avatar development. Incorporating these features would allow the avatar to reflect a wider range of user’s fitness habits rather than relying exclusively on workout activities, bringing the application closer to its original design concept.
\end{itemize}
